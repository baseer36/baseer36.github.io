Hand-knotted vs hand-tufted: what you are actually paying for

Both are described as handmade, and both are. But one lasts three generations and the other lasts about ten years, and the price gap is not arbitrary.

8 min read·Naqsh Carpets
In this guide

  • Hand-tufted: the rug is glued together
  • How to tell them apart in thirty seconds
  • What each one is actually good for

Hand-knotted: the rug is the structure

On a hand-knotted rug, a weaver sits at a loom strung with vertical cotton or wool threads — the warp. She ties a short length of yarn around pairs of warp threads, one knot at a time, cuts it, and moves across the row. At the end of each row she passes a horizontal weft thread through and beats it down hard with a comb. Then she starts the next row.

A 200 × 300 cm rug at 250,000 knots per square metre contains about fifteen million individual knots. At a realistic working pace that is somewhere between eight and fourteen months of one person's labour. This is why hand-knotted rugs cost what they cost: you are buying a year of someone's life, and there is no way to speed it up.

The important structural point is that the knots are the rug. There is no backing holding anything in place — the pile and the foundation are a single interlocked textile. Cut a hand-knotted rug in half and both halves are still rugs. That is why they can be repaired, re-fringed, re-edged and re-piled indefinitely, and why antique examples two hundred years old are still in daily use.

Hand-tufted: the rug is glued together

A hand-tufted rug starts as a plain cotton cloth stretched on a frame, with the design printed onto it. The maker uses a handheld tufting gun to punch loops of yarn through the cloth, following the printed lines. The loops are not knotted and nothing holds them in — at this stage, pulling one would draw the yarn straight out.

So the back is then coated with latex adhesive, and a second cloth backing is glued over the top to hide it. The latex is what holds the pile in. A 200 × 300 cm hand-tufted rug takes a skilled maker a few days rather than a year.

This is a legitimate way to make a rug and we sell plenty of them. It produces clean lines, carved detail and modern designs at a quarter of the hand-knotted price. But the honest limitation is the latex: it is a consumable. Over roughly eight to fifteen years it oxidises, hardens and begins to crumble. When that happens the rug sheds badly, the backing separates, and it cannot be repaired economically.

How to tell them apart in thirty seconds

Turn the rug over. That is the whole test.

Hand-knottedHand-tufted
BackThe pattern is visible on the back, as clear as the frontA plain cloth backing — you cannot see the design
EdgesFringe is an extension of the warp, structuralFringe is sewn or glued on as a separate strip
Feel of the backTextured, knotty, slightly irregularSmooth cloth, sometimes with a rubbery smell
Knot countQuoted and meaningfulNot applicable — there are no knots
The fringe tells you everythingOn a hand-knotted rug, the fringe is the warp threads themselves, emerging from the structure. On a hand-tufted rug, the fringe is a separate decorative strip sewn to the edge. If you can see a seam where the fringe meets the rug, it is tufted.

What each one is actually good for

This is not a ranking. They are different products solving different problems.

  • Choose hand-knotted when you want the rug to outlast the room — a formal living room, a majlis, a dining room, anything you expect to move house with. Also when you want fine curved detail, which tufting cannot render at the same resolution.
  • Choose hand-tufted when you want a specific modern design, a carved or high-low texture, a large size on a sensible budget, or a rug for a room you may redecorate in five years. Children's rooms, home offices and guest bedrooms are good tufted territory.

Note what the table shows about flatweaves: a good kilim costs less than a hand-tufted rug and outlives it comfortably, because there is no latex to fail. For a hallway or a dining room that is frequently the smartest money on the page.

Two things the trade will not always tell you

"Handmade" means nothing on its own. It is used for both constructions, and for machine-woven rugs that were hand-finished. Ask specifically: is it hand-knotted, hand-tufted, flatwoven or power-loomed?

Knot count is only meaningful within a type. A 400,000-knot Isfahan is finer than a 250,000-knot Tabriz. But knot count tells you nothing about wool quality, and a dense rug in poor wool will look worse in five years than a looser rug in good wool. Ask what the wool is and where it came from.
